The New IT

Three years is not far away. But technology can change a lot in three years.

What happens when software stops being something humans only write — and becomes something humans increasingly direct?

01

Why 2030?

A near horizon, not a distant myth

FACT Students entering computing programmes in the mid-2020s will be early-career workers in 2030. The horizon is a workforce cycle, not science fiction.

TREND Generative AI is already inside editors, support tools, and office software. The practical question is how far assistance goes, who reviews it, and who is liable when it fails—not whether the tools exist.

TREND Automation has long targeted routine digital work. Language models widen the set of tasks that look “routine enough” to draft: boilerplate code, first-pass documents, L1 replies. That does not automatically delete occupations; it can change the mix of tasks inside them.

PROJECTION Cloud, specialized chips, energy, cybersecurity, and robotics condition what is even feasible. A model cannot scale if inference is too expensive, too insecure, or too physically unconstrained. 2030 IT is therefore a stack problem, not only a chatbot problem.

This page synthesizes current trajectories with scenario thinking. It does not claim that any company will dominate 2030, or that AI will definitely eliminate jobs.

04

Ecosystem

Who shapes the technology stack?

Influence means a role in the stack—not a prediction of 2030 market control. No share figures are invented here.

AI companies

Model and product layer

Organizations such as OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Anthropic, Meta, Mistral, and others currently publish or productize foundation models. FACT They influence interfaces, safety policies, and what “default generation” looks like in 2026. They do not automatically own 2030.

Cloud providers

Delivery, identity, regions

Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and regional providers rent compute, storage, and identity. TREND Training and inference already concentrate demand on their GPUs and power contracts. Sovereign and hybrid variants may grow; that is a trajectory, not a crowned winner.

Chip companies

Silicon constraint

NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, TSMC, ARM, and others sit under the software story. FACT Accelerator supply and fabrication capacity already shape who can train large systems. Software strategy follows silicon access more than slogans.

Enterprise software

Where work actually happens

Firms such as Microsoft, Salesforce, SAP, ServiceNow, and Atlassian embed assistants into the systems employees already log into. Influence here is workflow, records, and compliance—not only demos.

Consumer technology

Devices and attention

Apple, Samsung, Google, Meta, and similar firms shape phones, headsets, and default apps. Spatial computing and on-device models, if they spread, will travel through this layer.

Cybersecurity

Trust vendors and public agencies

Companies such as CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, and Microsoft, plus CERTs and standards bodies, influence detection, identity, and disclosure norms. If generated software volume rises, this layer could matter more, not less.

05

Opportunity fields

Where complementary work could grow

These are reasons a field could matter—not a guaranteed job board for 2030.

AI operations

Serving, evaluating, and cost-controlling models is operational work. If models become infrastructure, someone still runs the plant.

AI evaluation

Capability without tests is theatre. Evaluation design could be how organizations decide what is safe enough to use.

AI governance

Policy, logging, and role design turn principles into daily practice—especially where regulation already exists.

Cybersecurity

Faster code can mean faster defects and new attack paths (including against models themselves).

AI safety

Misuse, loss of control in agents, and high-stakes errors are research and engineering problems, not only slogans.

AI auditing

Independent checks of data, evals, and claims could become as ordinary as financial audit is for accounts.

Human-centered automation

Designing which steps stay human prevents “automate first, notice harm later.”

Robotics

Physical work still needs integration, safety cases, and maintenance in structured environments.

Spatial computing

If overlays become professional tools, interaction design and accessibility become IT-adjacent crafts.

Quantum-safe security

Crypto-migration planning can matter even if useful quantum computers remain narrow. This is risk management, not gadget fandom.

AI + healthcare / finance / education

Domain errors are costly. Specialists who can refuse a bad suggestion may be more valuable than generic prompt users.

AI + climate / energy

Data-center power and optimization of energy systems are already coupled to digital infrastructure. That coupling could tighten.

08

Classification

Likely / possible / uncertain

Likely

Supported by current deployment: wider AI assistance in writing and coding; cloud as a default delivery layer; cybersecurity remaining first-order; humans remaining legally and operationally accountable for shipped software in most jurisdictions.

Possible

Bounded agents in support and test loops; fewer entry-level seats for boilerplate work; studio-style education; quantum-safe migration programmes; professional AR in training and field work.

Uncertain

Full collapse of junior employment; general humanoid labor; everyday quantum laptops; a single firm “owning” 2030; artificial general intelligence on a calendar.

09

Methodology

How did we think about 2030?

This exhibition combines current technology trends, public industry and policy reports, documented product deployments, and scenario planning. Scenarios are tools for asking “what if,” not forecasts.

  1. Stanford HAI, AI Index Report (annual) — capability, investment, and incident tracking. Used as a FACT/TREND baseline, not a 2030 prophecy.
  2. NIST, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) — a governance vocabulary (map, measure, manage, govern).
  3. World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs reports — employer surveys about skill demand. Treat as TREND/opinion of respondents, not destiny.
  4. OECD and ILO publications on AI and work — task-based analysis of automation exposure.
  5. IEA commentary on data-centre electricity — energy as a real constraint on scale.
  6. Official EU AI Act texts — example of binding public rules already in motion (FACT of law, not of 2030 outcomes).

Where this site shows numbers on the next page, they are labeled illustrative scenario data and must not be cited as empirical findings.
